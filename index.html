Current work & future directions

Research Vision

Understanding how microglia respond to aging and disease—and what enables them to remain functional.

My current research focuses on the aging brain. I investigate how immune signaling, lipid metabolism, lysosomal function, and changes in cellular state influence microglial responses in aging and Alzheimer’s disease.

I combine genetics, molecular profiling, spatial biology, and longitudinal analyses to examine how these responses vary across cells, brain regions, and time. The goal is to identify mechanisms that preserve function and determine when protective responses become harmful.

Building on this work, I aim to study resilience in a wider range of physiological states, including sleep and hibernation. These future directions ask whether mechanisms of stress tolerance and recovery can inform new approaches to brain aging and disease.

Core Scientific Questions

1. Adaptation — What preserves function under stress?

  • How do cells and tissues adapt to aging, environmental stress, and disease?
  • Which adaptive programs preserve function, and which lead to dysfunction?
  • How do genetic variation and environmental factors shape resilience across the lifespan?

2. Plasticity — What makes cellular changes reversible?

  • How do lipid metabolism, lysosomal function, and proteostasis regulate cellular resilience?
  • Why do some cells, tissues, and brain regions remain resilient while others become vulnerable?
  • How do metabolic and cellular state transitions influence aging, regeneration, and disease progression?

3. Measurement — Can molecular changes predict functional outcomes?

  • How can we move beyond chronological age to quantify dynamic biological states?
  • What biomarkers best capture adaptation, resilience, and reversibility?
  • Can aging trajectories be redirected through targeted biological interventions?

4. Translation — What can natural models of resilience teach us?

  • How has evolution shaped exceptional resilience to aging and environmental stress?
  • What can we learn from hibernation, exceptional longevity, regeneration, and natural resistance to extreme environments?
  • Can these adaptive programs be translated or engineered to promote healthy aging and disease resistance?

Research Support

Ongoing

Identifying Resilience Mechanisms in Aging and AD through Microglial Intervention

Project Period: 09/11/2026 - 08/31/2028

Principal Investigator: Tsai AP

Sponsor: K99/R00 NIH Pathway to Independence Award, National Institute on Aging

Project Description: Microglial dysfunction, characterized by lipid droplet accumulation, lysosomal deficits, and impaired debris clearance, is a shared hallmark of brain aging and Alzheimer’s disease (AD), yet the mechanisms that sustain microglial resilience remain poorly understood.

These studies will advance our understanding of the molecular and spatial mechanisms that underlie microglial resilience and may reveal novel therapeutic targets to preserve brain health and cognitive function in aging and AD.

Ongoing

Uncovering Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s disease-specific transcripts through single-cell long-read sequencing

Project Period: 10/15/2025 - 03/31/2027

Principal Investigator: Tsai AP

Sponsor: Stanford Alzheimer’s Disease Research Center

Project Description: The objective of this project is to identify Alzheimer’s disease- and Parkinson’s disease-specific immune transcript isoforms using single-cell long-read RNA sequencing. By integrating genetic risk variants, transcript isoform diversity, and disease-relevant immune states, this work aims to uncover previously unrecognized molecular mechanisms underlying neurodegeneration and to establish a foundation for immune-targeted therapeutic and biomarker development.

Completed

The role of an AD protective- and longevity-associated variant of PLCG2 in brain aging

Project Period: 07/01/2023 - 06/30/2026

Principal Investigator: Tsai AP

Sponsor: Larry L. Hillblom Foundation

Project Description: The primary objective of this project is to define how the Alzheimer’s disease protective and longevity-associated PLCG2 P522R variant preserves brain resilience during aging. This work investigates genetic, cellular, and molecular mechanisms by which PLCG2 signaling maintains microglial function, limits age-associated neuropathology, and promotes healthy brain aging.

Completed

An effective therapeutic approach for Alzheimer's disease by targeting phospholipase C gamma 2

Project Period: 01/01/2021 - 12/31/2021

Principal Investigator: Tsai AP

Sponsor: Indiana Clinical and Translational Sciences Institute

Project Description: The objective of this project was to elucidate the role of PLCG2-mediated microglial signaling in Alzheimer’s disease pathogenesis. This study focused on the regulation of microglial function through the BTK-PLCG2 signaling axis, providing mechanistic insight into how AD-associated genetic variation influences neuroinflammation and disease progression.

Future Directions

I aim to develop my independent research program around three goals:

(1) Identify mechanisms that help microglia preserve function during aging and neurodegeneration.

(2) Connect molecular measurements over time with cellular states, functional changes, and recovery.

(3) Test whether protective mechanisms found in comparative physiological models can inform studies of brain health.

These goals connect my established work in neuroimmunology with a longer-term interest in how living systems tolerate stress and recover.

Purpose & approach

Mission & Values

Mission

Understand how cells preserve function under stress, why this capacity declines with age, and how it might be restored.

I pursue this mission through research on microglia, brain aging, and Alzheimer’s disease. My longer-term goal is to connect these studies with natural models of stress tolerance and recovery, and to test whether the mechanisms they reveal can inform approaches to brain health.

Five principles guide my work

01

Investigate mechanisms

I use genetics, perturbation experiments, and molecular measurements to distinguish associations from causal explanations. Biomarkers are most useful when they help explain what cells are doing and why.

02

Study protection and recovery

I ask what preserves function as well as what drives disease. Natural models, including hibernation and exceptional longevity, offer questions to test about stress tolerance, plasticity, and recovery.

03

Connect biological scales

I combine molecular, cellular, spatial, and longitudinal approaches to understand how local changes relate to tissue function and outcomes over time.

04

Make the evidence trustworthy

I prioritize careful experimental design, appropriate controls, transparent reporting, and reproducible analysis. Ambitious ideas require clear tests and conclusions that match the evidence.

05

Support people and independence

I value a welcoming research community where people can ask questions, challenge assumptions, and develop their own ideas. Through mentorship and collaboration, I aim to help trainees build confidence, skills, and scientific independence.
